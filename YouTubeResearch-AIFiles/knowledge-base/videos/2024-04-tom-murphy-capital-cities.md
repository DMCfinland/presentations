# Tom Murphy (Warren Buffett's Favorite Manager)

## Summary
Tom Murphy and Dan Burke transformed Capital Cities Broadcasting from a tiny five-station operation into a media empire worth 3x CBS's value over 30 years—despite starting 16x smaller. Their formula: extreme cost discipline ("forever cost conscious"), radical decentralization ("delegates to the point of anarchy"), selective acquisitions in known businesses, and relentless focus on operational margins. Buffett called them "the greatest two-person combination in management the world has ever seen." This is the definitive case study of how operational excellence creates acquisition arbitrage and how a "rowboat" beats a "Queen Elizabeth 2" through superior management.

## 1. Context

**Background:**
In 1966, when Tom Murphy became CEO of Capital Cities at age 40, the company was a tiny broadcaster with 5 TV stations and 4 radio stations—all in small markets. CBS, the dominant media company, had a market cap 16x larger. Murphy had started 11 years earlier with zero broadcast experience, hired at age 29 to turn around a bankrupt Albany TV station. He recruited Dan Burke (also with no broadcast experience) in 1961. By 1995, when Murphy sold to Disney for $19 billion, Capital Cities was worth 3x CBS.

Warren Buffett described their rivalry as "a transatlantic race between a rowboat and the QE2"—and the rowboat won decisively. Buffett called Murphy his hero, said Murphy made him a better person, and credited Murphy with teaching him critical lessons about cost control and patience.

**Why This Matters:**
This is the definitive proof that management quality matters more than starting position, industry tailwinds, or scale advantages. Murphy's 30-year track record demonstrates that:
1. Operational excellence creates acquisition arbitrage (buying assets and improving them faster than peers)
2. Extreme decentralization scales better than centralized control
3. Cost discipline compounds into an insurmountable competitive advantage
4. Patient, selective capital allocation beats aggressive empire-building

Every principle Murphy used contradicted the "conventional wisdom" of the conglomerate era—yet his results spoke for themselves.

**Key Stats:**
- $1 invested with Murphy in 1966 → $204 by 1995 (29-year CAGR of ~19.9%)
- Started 16x smaller than CBS → ended 3x larger than CBS
- Bought ABC in 1985 for $3.5 billion (100%+ of Capital Cities' enterprise value)—the largest non-oil transaction in history at the time
- Share buybacks: $1.8 billion spent, generating 22.4% returns over 19 years
- Improved ABC TV stations' margins from 30% to 50%+ in just 2 years
- Headquarters staff for entire publishing division (6 daily newspapers, several magazines, weekly shoppers): 3 people

## 2. Vision & Why

**Core Mission:**
"The goal is not to have the longest train but to arrive at the station first using the least fuel." (Tom Murphy)

Murphy's vision was anti-growth for growth's sake. While CBS and other conglomerates measured success by size and diversification, Murphy measured success by value creation per dollar of capital deployed. The mission was to build the most valuable media company—not the largest.

**The "Why" Behind It:**
Murphy believed in three core principles:
1. **Focus over diversification** — Know your business deeply; don't chase fashion into unrelated industries
2. **Operations drive value** — Superior margins create competitive advantage in acquisitions and compound over time
3. **Patience beats aggression** — Wait for the right opportunities; say no to 99% of deals

As Murphy told Thorndike: "The business of business is a lot of little decisions every day mixed up with a very few big decisions." This reflects his belief that excellence comes from daily operational discipline, not just brilliant strategy.

**Enduring Nature:**
This vision never changed in 30 years. Murphy acquired only in known media businesses (TV, radio, newspapers, cable). He never diversified into toys, sports teams, or unrelated ventures like CBS did. The formula was simple and permanent:
1. Buy media properties in attractive markets
2. Improve operations to industry-leading margins
3. Pay down debt
4. Repeat

The vision endured because it was grounded in competitive advantage, not trends.

## 3. Strategic Engine

**How They Actually Won:**
Capital Cities created a **rollup-with-operational-leverage model**:

1. **Generate excess cash** through industry-leading margins (50%+ vs. industry average of 30%)
2. **Use leverage selectively** to acquire attractive media properties
3. **Improve operations immediately** under Burke's oversight (often doubling margins within 2 years)
4. **Pay down debt** with improved cash flow
5. **Repeat** — use the now-deleveraged asset as collateral for the next acquisition

This created a compounding machine: each acquisition made the next one easier because Capital Cities had:
- **Lower effective acquisition cost** (they knew they'd improve margins fast)
- **More credibility** with sellers (track record of operational excellence)
- **Better financing terms** (consistent cash generation)

Murphy described it simply: "We take the assets and once we've paid them off, we leverage them again to buy other assets."

**Key Components:**
1. **Extreme cost discipline** — "Forever cost conscious" was embedded in every decision, from painting only road-facing sides of buildings to eliminating corporate limos
2. **Radical decentralization** — Headquarters was "anorexic"; field managers had full autonomy as long as they hit margin targets
3. **Selective, large acquisitions** — Murphy only did a handful of major deals (each 25%+ of market cap), spending years cultivating relationships with sellers
4. **Aggressive share buybacks** — During bear markets (mid-1970s to early 1980s), Murphy bought back ~50% of shares at single-digit P/E multiples
5. **Division of labor** — Burke handled operations; Murphy handled capital allocation. No overlap, total trust.

**Why This Worked:**
The engine worked because **operational excellence created acquisition arbitrage**:
- CBS paid full price for assets and ran them at 30% margins
- Capital Cities paid full price but ran them at 50%+ margins, effectively paying 60% of what CBS paid per dollar of cash flow
- This meant Capital Cities could outbid anyone and still generate superior returns

Additionally, extreme decentralization meant Capital Cities could scale without bureaucracy. While CBS had 42 presidents and vice presidents, Capital Cities' entire publishing division had 3 HQ staff. Low overhead → high margins → more cash for acquisitions → repeat.

## 4. Culture & Incentives

**Cultural Principles:**
Murphy distilled the culture into one paragraph, printed inside every annual report:

"Decentralization is the cornerstone of our philosophy. Our goal is to hire the best people we can and give them the responsibility and authority they need to perform their jobs. We expect our managers to be forever cost conscious and to recognize and exploit sales potential."

The culture had three non-negotiable elements:

1. **Forever cost conscious** — Costs were scrutinized obsessively. Murphy taught Buffett to view hiring decisions as "$3 million decisions" (lifetime cost including raises, benefits, and yes, toilet paper), not "$20,000 decisions." Managers who got sloppy about costs didn't last.

2. **Extreme autonomy** — Field managers ran their properties like owners. They rarely heard from New York if they hit their numbers. As Burke said, "Murphy delegates to the point of anarchy."

3. **Leadership by example** — Murphy was a "cabman, not a limo man." When ABC executives (a "limousine culture") saw Murphy taking cabs everywhere, the limo culture disappeared within months. As Murphy said, "Is there any other way?" (to lead besides by example).

**Incentive Structure:**
- Managers were judged on **local market dominance** (advertising share) and **margins relative to peers**
- Quarterly meetings focused line-by-line on capital expenditures and expenses
- Profit margins were "a form of report card to HQ"
- Managers who hit targets were left alone; those who missed them got scrutiny, not punishment (unless incompetent)
- Long-term employee ownership encouraged (low turnover was a badge of honor)

There's no evidence of complex incentive schemes. The system was simple: hit your margin targets, grow local market share, keep costs low, and you'll be rewarded with autonomy and responsibility.

**Alignment Mechanisms:**
1. **Hiring for alignment** — Murphy and Burke hired "young foxes" with "intelligence, ability, and drive" over industry experience. They wanted people who'd thrive in autonomy, not people who needed hand-holding.

2. **Cultural selection** — A rival broadcaster remarked, "We see lots of resumés but we never see any from Capital Cities." Why? "The system in place corrupts you with so much autonomy and authority that you can't imagine leaving."

3. **Annual report clarity** — The culture was explicit and public, printed in every annual report. No ambiguity about what mattered.

4. **Leadership consistency** — Murphy and Burke worked together for 30 years. The culture never wavered because the leaders never changed.

One story illustrates the culture: At a management retreat, a bartender invested in Capital Cities stock in the early 1970s and made a handsome return. When asked why, he said, "I've worked a lot of corporate events over the years but Capital Cities was the only company where you couldn't tell who the bosses were." Zero hierarchy, zero pretension.

## 5. Resource & Capital Allocation

**Where Money Flowed:**
Murphy had two primary uses of capital:

1. **Selective acquisitions (majority of capital)** — Murphy spent years cultivating relationships with sellers, then moved boldly when opportunities arose. He focused on:
   - TV and radio stations (core business)
   - Newspapers (similar economics: advertising-driven, high margins, strong barriers)
   - Cable TV (embraced as opportunity, not threat)
   - ABC Network (1985, the "master stroke" — $3.5 billion, 100%+ of enterprise value)

2. **Share buybacks ($1.8 billion, 22.4% returns)** — During the mid-1970s to early 1980s bear market, Murphy bought back ~50% of outstanding shares at single-digit P/E multiples. He later said, "I only wish I had bought more."

Capital allocation was **lumpy and patient**: long periods of inactivity (just paying down debt, improving operations) punctuated by very large bets.

**What They DIDN'T Spend On:**
- **Diversification** — Never bought unrelated businesses (no toys, no sports teams, no oil companies like CBS did)
- **Fancy headquarters** — No palace (Charlie Munger: "Why does a newspaper need a palace to publish in?")
- **Corporate staff** — Headquarters was "anorexic": no VPs of marketing, strategic planning, or HR; no corporate counsel; no PR department
- **Limos and perks** — Murphy took cabs; executives flew coach; offices were functional, not luxurious
- **Auctions** — Murphy typically bid 60-70% below winning bids at auctions. He'd walk away rather than overpay.
- **Dividends** — Paid minor dividends but retained most cash for better uses (acquisitions, buybacks)

Murphy's allocation philosophy: "I get paid not just to make deals but to make good deals." He'd wait years for the right opportunity. Patience was a competitive advantage.

**Allocation Philosophy:**
Murphy viewed every dollar as a **permanent capital commitment**. The "$3 million hiring decision" story exemplifies this: a $20,000 assistant becomes a $3 million lifetime commitment once you factor in raises, benefits, and other costs. And once hired, they're unlikely to be fired.

This philosophy created extreme discipline:
- **High bar for spending** — Every expense had to justify itself over its lifetime, not just this year
- **Leverage as a tool, not a trap** — Debt was used to buy assets, then paid down quickly. Capital Cities never over-levered.
- **Focus on ROIC, not growth** — Revenue growth was good only if it improved returns on invested capital

Murphy believed cost control was the only thing you could fully control: "You can't control your revenues; you can control your costs." This was critical in advertising-driven businesses with cyclical revenue.

## 6. Moats & Time Horizon

**Competitive Advantages:**

1. **Operational excellence moat** — Capital Cities consistently ran at 50%+ margins while competitors ran at 30%. This created:
   - Acquisition arbitrage (could pay full price and still generate superior returns)
   - Financial resilience (more buffer during downturns)
   - Compounding advantage (more cash to reinvest)

2. **Cultural moat** — The "system that corrupts you with autonomy" created extreme loyalty. Low turnover meant:
   - Accumulated knowledge and relationships in local markets
   - Consistency in operations (same managers for decades)
   - Attraction of top talent (people wanted to work there)

3. **Scale + focus moat** — Over time, Capital Cities became the best operator in its specific niches:
   - Local TV stations: dominant in their markets
   - Newspapers: high margins, strong local franchises
   - Cable: early mover, understood the opportunity
   - This reputation made sellers prefer Capital Cities (even at lower prices) because they trusted Murphy to run the business well

4. **Capital allocation moat** — Murphy's discipline meant Capital Cities always had dry powder when opportunities arose. During recessions, they bought aggressively (share buybacks, acquisitions) while competitors were overleveraged.

**Time Horizon:**
Murphy played an **infinite game** (30-year tenure; sold only when it made strategic sense to shareholders). His moats were designed to compound forever:
- Operational margins improve slowly but permanently
- Cultural systems entrench over decades
- Local market dominance creates network effects

The ABC acquisition (1985) came 30 years into his career. As he told Thorndike, he'd been training his entire life for that moment. This reflects a long-term mindset: build capabilities slowly, then deploy them boldly.

**Why Time Was Their Friend:**
1. **Compounding margins** — Every year, Capital Cities' cost discipline widened the margin gap with competitors. By year 20, it was insurmountable.

2. **Reputation compounding** — Each successful acquisition made the next one easier (sellers trusted them, lenders gave better terms).

3. **Cultural deepening** — The longer people worked at Capital Cities, the more they internalized the culture. Turnover was near zero.

4. **Patience as filter** — Murphy's willingness to wait years for the right deal meant he avoided the disasters that befell impatient competitors (bad acquisitions, over-leveraging).

Buffett's description of the "rowboat beating the QE2" captures this: over short distances, size and power matter. Over long distances, efficiency and persistence win.

## 7. Flywheels & Lock-In

**Primary Flywheel:**
Capital Cities' flywheel was a **rollup-with-operational-leverage loop**:

**Flywheel Visualization:**
```
Generate excess cash (50%+ margins)
    ↓
Leverage assets to acquire new properties selectively
    ↓
Improve operations immediately (Burke's oversight)
    ↓
Pay down debt with improved cash flow
    ↓
Now-deleveraged asset becomes collateral for next acquisition
    ↓
Reputation improves → sellers prefer Capital Cities → better deal flow
    ↓
[Back to top: Even more cash to deploy, stronger reputation, better opportunities]
```

Each turn of the flywheel made the next turn easier:
- More cash → bigger acquisitions possible
- Better reputation → more deal flow, better terms
- More experience → faster operational improvements
- Larger scale → better negotiating position

**Secondary Flywheel: Culture**
```
Hire "young foxes" with intelligence, ability, drive
    ↓
Give them extreme autonomy + clear cost expectations
    ↓
They thrive and generate industry-leading margins
    ↓
Word spreads: "best place to work if you're talented"
    ↓
Top talent seeks out Capital Cities (even at lower pay)
    ↓
[Back to top: Even better hires, even stronger culture]
```

**Lock-In Mechanisms:**

1. **Employee lock-in** — "The system corrupts you with so much autonomy and authority that you can't imagine leaving." Once managers experienced true autonomy, hierarchical bureaucracies felt suffocating. Zero turnover.

2. **Seller lock-in** — Once Capital Cities acquired a property and improved it, sellers of similar properties wanted to work with Murphy (even at lower prices) because they trusted him to preserve what they'd built.

3. **Market lock-in** — Capital Cities' focus on local market dominance (especially in local news for TV stations) created network effects: #1 station → more ad revenue → more resources for content → stronger #1 position.

4. **Cost structure lock-in** — Once you've embedded "forever cost conscious" culture, reversing it is nearly impossible. Competitors couldn't match Capital Cities' margins without years of painful restructuring.

**Compounding Effect:**
The flywheel accelerated because:
- Murphy and Burke's partnership was stable for 30 years (no leadership turnover to reset the culture)
- Each acquisition added to both scale and reputation, making the next one easier
- Time deepened the cultural moat (new hires learned from 20-year veterans)

By the 1980s, Capital Cities' flywheel was spinning so fast that Murphy could acquire ABC (a company larger than Capital Cities) and integrate it successfully. CBS, despite starting 16x larger, never built a comparable flywheel because they lacked operational discipline and cultural coherence.

## 8. Stakeholder Alignment

**Winners (Win-Win-Win):**

1. **Shareholders** — $1 invested in 1966 → $204 by 1995. This is among the greatest wealth creation stories in business history. Buffett invested in Capital Cities and became a close friend of Murphy's, calling him a hero.

2. **Employees** — Managers had extraordinary autonomy, low turnover, and long careers. Many became wealthy through stock ownership. The bartender story (investing in the early 1970s and making a "handsome return") shows even peripheral observers could benefit.

3. **Local communities** — Capital Cities invested in local content (especially news) to dominate local markets. This meant better-funded local journalism and programming than competitors could afford.

4. **Sellers** — Murphy's reputation for operational excellence meant sellers trusted him to preserve their legacy (important for family-owned newspapers and stations). He often got deals by asking sellers what they thought was fair and agreeing immediately if reasonable.

5. **Buffett/Disney** — Buffett financed the ABC deal and profited massively. Disney acquired Capital Cities in 1995 for $19 billion, gaining valuable assets and inheriting strong management (Bob Iger, hired by Murphy at 37, later became Disney CEO).

**Losers:**

1. **CBS and other competitors** — Capital Cities' operational excellence made competitors look incompetent. CBS started 16x larger and ended 1/3 the size. Other media conglomerates that followed "conventional wisdom" (diversification, centralized control, empire-building) were left behind.

2. **Corporate staff who craved bureaucracy** — If you wanted a big corporate staff, fancy titles, limos, and palace headquarters, Capital Cities was hell. The ABC acquisition was a "culture clash" because ABC was a "limousine culture" and Murphy was a "cabman."

3. **Investment bankers** — Murphy never used investment bankers for acquisitions, preferring direct relationships with sellers. This cost bankers millions in fees.

4. **Sellers at auctions** — Murphy walked away from auctions, typically bidding 60-70% below winning bids. Sellers who needed top dollar lost out.

**Ethical Considerations:**
There are no significant ethical red flags in Murphy's approach. The culture was demanding but fair:
- High autonomy balanced high accountability
- Cost discipline was rational, not abusive (Murphy didn't cut investments that drove local market dominance)
- No evidence of mistreating employees, communities, or sellers

The phrase "forever cost conscious" might raise concerns about cutting corners, but the principle was "careful, not cheap." Capital Cities invested heavily in content and talent when it drove market leadership.

One critique: extreme cost discipline can become penny-wise-pound-foolish if taken too far (e.g., not investing in growth opportunities to save costs). However, Murphy avoided this trap by focusing on local market share and margins as joint goals, not cost-cutting in isolation.

## 9. North Star Metric

**What They Optimized For:**
**Local market profit margin** (relative to industry peers) and **local advertising market share**

Murphy and Burke believed margins were "a form of report card to HQ." Quarterly meetings scrutinized margins line-by-line. The goal was simple:
- Run at 50%+ margins (vs. industry average of 30%)
- Dominate local advertising markets (especially local news for TV stations)

**Why This Metric:**
Margins were chosen because:
1. **Controllable** — You can't control revenue (advertising is cyclical), but you can control costs. Margins are the one thing management fully controls.

2. **Compounding** — High margins → more cash → more acquisitions → more scale → even higher margins. Margins compound over time.

3. **Relative, not absolute** — Murphy measured margins relative to peers, not absolute levels. This created a competitive dynamic: even if industry margins fell, Capital Cities stayed ahead.

4. **Simplicity** — Everyone understood it. No complex scorecards. Just: hit your margin target and grow local share.

**Why local advertising market share mattered:**
Capital Cities learned early that the #1 TV station in local news captured a disproportionate share of advertising revenue. This created a clear strategy: invest in local content to dominate, then extract high margins.

**How They Measured:**
- **Quarterly meetings** — Publishers and station managers came to New York and reviewed margins line-by-line with Murphy and Burke. Particular attention was paid to capital expenditures and expenses.

- **Peer benchmarking** — Managers were expected to outperform their peers. If your margin was 45% and the peer average was 40%, you were doing well. If it was 35%, you had explaining to do.

- **Local market share tracking** — Advertising share was tracked obsessively. Dominance in local news was the leading indicator.

Outside of these meetings, managers were left alone. As Burke said after taking over the Albany station, he initially sent weekly memos to Murphy. After several months of no response, he stopped—realizing his time was better spent on local operations. The message: hit your numbers, and we won't bother you.

### Non-Obvious Insights

- **"Forever cost conscious" doesn't mean cheap—it means careful.** Murphy invested heavily in local news to dominate local advertising markets. He cut corporate overhead, not strategic investments. The principle is: spend aggressively on things that drive competitive advantage, ruthlessly on everything else.

- **Decentralization scales better than centralization for rollups.** Capital Cities' "anorexic" HQ meant they could integrate acquisitions without bloating overhead. CBS had 42 presidents and vice presidents; Capital Cities' entire publishing division (6 daily newspapers + magazines) had 3 HQ staff. Low overhead → high margins → more acquisitions → repeat.

- **Operational excellence creates acquisition arbitrage.** Because Capital Cities ran at 50%+ margins vs. 30% industry average, they could pay full price for assets and still generate higher returns than competitors. This meant they won deals without needing to be the highest bidder.

- **Hiring for "intelligence, ability, and drive" beats hiring for experience.** Neither Murphy nor Burke had broadcast experience when hired. They proved that raw talent + autonomy > industry expertise. This became their hiring philosophy: target "young foxes" with fresh perspectives over industry veterans with ingrained habits.

- **Patience as competitive advantage.** Murphy's willingness to wait years for the right acquisition meant he avoided the traps that killed competitors (bad deals, over-leveraging, diversification into unrelated businesses). As he said, "I get paid not just to make deals but to make good deals." Saying no 99% of the time is a superpower.

- **The "cabman vs. limousine culture" story reveals leadership power.** When ABC executives (a "limousine culture") saw Murphy taking cabs, the limo culture disappeared within months. No memos, no policies—just example. As Murphy said, "Is there any other way?" True culture change happens through visible, consistent leadership behavior, not HR programs.

- **Leverage is a tool, not a lifestyle.** Murphy used debt aggressively to fund acquisitions (the ABC deal was 100%+ of enterprise value), then paid it down rapidly. Debt was a bridge to the next level of scale, not a permanent state. This kept Capital Cities flexible and antifragile.

- **Margins are a "report card to HQ."** By making margins the North Star, Murphy aligned incentives perfectly: cost control = good grades, bloat = bad grades. Simple, transparent, measurable. Managers knew exactly what mattered.

- **The "$3 million hiring decision" reframes resource allocation.** Murphy's advice to Buffett—thinking of a $20K hire as a $3M lifetime commitment—illustrates how the best capital allocators think. Every decision has compounding effects. This mental model prevents casual spending.

- **Rollups fail because of integration, not acquisition.** As Thorndike notes, most rollups "collapsed under the burden of too much debt" or "underestimated the difficulty of integrating acquisitions and improving operations." Capital Cities succeeded because Burke was a world-class operator who could integrate and improve acquisitions rapidly. Strategy without operational excellence fails.

- **"Young foxes" with autonomy compound into cultural moats.** By hiring talented young people and giving them extreme responsibility early (Burke hired at 30, Bob Iger hired at 37 to run ABC entertainment), Murphy created a loyal, highly capable management team. These people never left because they'd never find comparable autonomy elsewhere. This loyalty became a moat.

- **Local market dominance > scale.** Capital Cities focused on being #1 in local news in their markets, even if the markets were small. This strategy (dominant local share) beat CBS's strategy (scale across everything). Network effects matter more at the local level than at the national level for advertising-driven businesses.

## 11. Application & Mental Model

### When to Use This Pattern

**This pattern works when:**

1. **You're in a fragmented industry with rollup potential** — Media in the 1960s-1990s was perfect: thousands of local TV/radio stations and newspapers, most family-owned, few professional operators. Today's equivalents: HVAC, plumbing, dental practices, car washes, etc.

2. **Operational improvement is possible** — Capital Cities' edge was taking 30% margin businesses to 50%+ margins. If you can't meaningfully improve operations, this model doesn't work. Look for industries with wide variance in operator quality.

3. **You have a world-class operator as partner** — Murphy without Burke would've struggled (capital allocation without operational excellence). Burke without Murphy would've stayed small (operations without capital allocation). The two-person combination is critical.

4. **Debt is available and assets are leverage-able** — The "buy, improve, lever up again" loop requires lenders who'll finance acquisitions against cash-flowing assets. Doesn't work if debt markets are frozen or assets are hard to value.

5. **You can be patient** — Murphy waited years for the right deals. If you're on a 3-year investment fund timeline or need to show growth every quarter, this model is frustrating. It requires infinite-game thinking.

6. **Cultural alignment matters to stakeholders** — Murphy's reputation for operational excellence meant sellers preferred him even at lower prices. If sellers only care about price (auction dynamics), this advantage disappears.

### When NOT to Use This Pattern

**This pattern fails when:**

1. **The industry is mature with professional operators** — Capital Cities thrived because most competitors were poorly managed. In industries where everyone is professional (e.g., modern FAANG companies), operational arbitrage doesn't exist.

2. **Technology or business models are rapidly changing** — Murphy's model worked because broadcasting economics were stable for decades. In fast-changing industries (e.g., crypto, AI), operational discipline matters less than strategic positioning.

3. **You lack operational expertise** — If you can't improve margins, you're just buying revenue. This becomes a commodity game. Capital Cities worked because Burke could reliably double margins within 2 years.

4. **Scale economies dominate** — Murphy's decentralized model worked because local broadcasting didn't require scale. In industries with massive scale economies (e.g., chip manufacturing, cloud computing), extreme decentralization is a liability.

5. **You're impatient or need to deploy capital quickly** — Private equity funds with 5-year horizons struggle with Murphy's patience. This model is for permanent capital (family offices, Berkshire Hathaway, founder-CEOs).

6. **Cultural fit doesn't matter** — If employees don't value autonomy (e.g., they prefer structure and hierarchy), Murphy's "delegate to anarchy" approach will create chaos, not performance.

**General Principles Extracted:**
