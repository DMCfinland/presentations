# I Spent 200 Hours Teaching AI Writing—Here Are 6 Principles Everyone Gets WRONG (+ Demo Prompt)

## Summary
The fundamental bottleneck in AI-assisted writing is not model capability but organizational ability to articulate what constitutes good work. AI forces tacit knowledge into explicit standards, requiring businesses to codify quality criteria that were previously held as instincts. The cost of AI generation has dropped to zero, making the premium now on communicating intent clearly. Organizations drowning in "AI slop" must shift from relying on "I know it when I see it" to concrete, testable specifications—and deploy AI on the evaluation side, not just generation.

## 1. Context

**Background:** Organizations are drowning in AI-generated business documents. While AI has dropped the cost of writing to nearly zero, most businesses lack the structured knowledge to specify what makes good writing. The presenter has spent 200+ hours teaching AI writing and built prompts/Claude skills to address systematic quality failures in business documentation (meeting notes, PRDs, memos, press releases, etc.).

**Why This Matters:** This represents a fundamental shift in organizational capability requirements. The competitive advantage is no longer "who can write fastest" but "who can articulate requirements most clearly." Companies that cannot make this transition will drown in low-quality AI output while competitors who master specification will scale quality consistently. This is a people problem disguised as a technology problem.

**Key Stats:**
- 200+ hours spent teaching AI writing
- Cost of business writing dropped to "nearly zero"
- Meeting notes example uses 5-7 failure modes as quality checks
- Organizations need to specify requirements "really, really clearly"

## 2. Vision & Why

**Core Mission:** Enable organizations to produce consistently high-quality business writing by making tacit knowledge about quality explicit and machine-readable. Transform the specification bottleneck from a constraint into a strategic capability.

**The "Why" Behind It:** Pre-AI, organizations relied on individuals having "instincts" for good work and learned through experience what templates meant. AI cannot read minds or develop instincts—it amplifies ambiguity rather than reducing it. The alternative to solving this is drowning forever in AI slop, because "everyone's using it and everybody I know at work that's drowning in AI docs, which is a lot of people. Well, that's not going to stop."

**Enduring Nature:** 
- **Timeless:** The need to communicate intent clearly, define quality standards, align structure with business logic, and evaluate systematically
- **Timeless:** Information architecture problems that businesses "just paper over" 
- **2024-2026 specific:** The particular AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT), the meeting notes transcription workflow, the current generation of models
- **Timeless principle becoming urgent:** AI forces the specification bottleneck into visibility—what was always there is now unavoidable

## 3. Strategic Engine

**How This Actually Works:** 
Organizations must convert implicit quality standards into explicit, testable specifications. These specifications become prompts that encode:
1. Document purpose and goal (decision enablement)
2. Business logic (not just template structure)
3. Required vs. prohibited elements (constraints)
4. Failure modes (what bad looks like)
5. Validation criteria (self-checking mechanisms)

AI then operates on both generation AND evaluation sides, with humans defining requirements and AI executing consistently against those requirements at scale.

**Key Components:**
1. **Explicit goal definition:** Every document must enable person X to make choice Y
2. **Information architecture:** Structure IS business logic, not decoration—defines what information serves which decisions
3. **Constraint specification:** Length limits, required ownership attribution, prohibited vagueness
4. **Failure mode documentation:** 5-7 examples of quality problems specific to document type
5. **Validation layer:** AI self-checks against quality criteria before output, with human evaluation prompts available

**Why This Works:** 
- AI is excellent at consistency once requirements are clear, but terrible at inferring unstated preferences
- Ambiguity compounds through generation (doesn't reduce)
- Organizations already had these quality problems—AI just exposes "information asymmetries, informational vagueness that previously hid in a lot of our writing because people wrote it and you just assumed that people were doing their best thinking"
- Scaling evaluation (not just generation) creates a feedback loop that improves specification quality over time

## 4. Behavioral Design

**Behavioral Principles:**
- **Specification forces clarity:** Requiring explicit standards forces organizations to articulate what they actually value, not what they claim to value
- **Critique becomes normalized:** "We now don't assume that everyone's doing their best thinking which means we critique more which is actually healthy"
- **Iteration requires diagnosis:** Users must learn to diagnose *why* output failed (specification problem) rather than blame the model
- **Evaluation precedes generation:** Teaching people to evaluate (using AI assist) improves their ability to specify

**Incentive Structure:**
- **Encourages:** Taking time upfront to define quality criteria, documenting failure modes, building reusable prompt libraries, treating prompts as product requirements
- **Discourages:** "Make it better" iteration without specification, relying on templates without business logic, assuming AI can reduce ambiguity, blaming the model for poor output
- **Removes:** The escape hatch of relying on individual instinct or "I know it when I see it"

**Alignment Mechanisms:**
- Validation checkpoints prevent output until quality criteria are met
- Failure mode examples create shared language for quality problems
- Claude skills/prompt libraries spread standards across organization
- Meeting notes structure (decisions/actions/owners) forces accountability
- Length constraints prevent AI verbosity

## 5. Time & Attention

**Where Time Flows:**
- **Upfront:** Defining document purpose, goals, structure as business logic, failure modes, quality criteria
- **Upfront (one-time):** Building prompt libraries and Claude skills for common document types
- **Saved:** Writing/editing time (AI generates), reading time (higher signal-to-noise), evaluation time (AI first-pass with clear criteria)
- **New allocation:** Specification refinement—using failure cases to improve prompts iteratively

**What This System DOESN'T Spend On:**
- Individual writers developing instincts through trial-and-error
- Reading low-quality AI slop to find signal
- Vague iteration cycles ("make it better")
- Inconsistent quality across team members
- Template-filling without thinking
- Assumed context that isn't explicit

**Allocation Philosophy:** 
"Do you have time for the business writing you're drowning in?" The choice isn't between spending time on specification vs. not spending time—it's between spending time upfront on specification OR spending far more time drowning in low-quality output. Time spent making tacit knowledge explicit is time that compounds across every future document. "There is no substitute for that intent. The AI won't bring it."

"You can have a really consistent high-quality bar and you can know whether someone is writing to that bar or not." Meanwhile, competitors who don't build this muscle keep drowning.

The gap between "organizations that can specify" and "organizations drowning in slop" widens exponentially because specification capability builds on itself while low-quality generation just creates more noise.

## 9. System Health Metric

**What to Optimize For:** **Information-to-Decision Latency** - The time between when information is documented and when it enables someone to make the intended decision, weighted by decision quality.

Alternative formulation: **Stakeholder Clarity Score** - Can the intended reader of this document make their decision without asking clarifying questions?

**Why This Metric:**
The purpose of business documents is decision enablement, not just information transfer. The presenter emphasizes: "Documents really ought to be written for goals and decisions... if you can't tell if this document enables person X to make choice Y... if you don't know what it looks like and why you're reading it, it's it was going to be useless anyway, right? But now we blame the AI."

This metric captures:
1. **Speed:** How quickly can decisions happen?
2. **Quality:** Are decisions well-informed?
3. **Clarity:** Is intent successfully communicated?
4. **Efficiency:** Are follow-up questions needed?

**How to Measure:**
**Quantitative proxies:**
- Track clarifying questions per document (lower is better)
- Measure decision cycle time before/after specification implementation
- Survey intended readers: "Could you make your decision based solely on this document?" (yes/no)
- Count AI evaluation passes needed before validation criteria pass (fewer is better over time as prompts improve)

**Qualitative assessment:**
- Review actual decisions made based on documents—were they well-informed or did they miss key context?
- Audit meeting notes → action items → completion pathway (do owners know what to do?)
- Test new employees: can they use documented specifications to produce quality output without extensive training?

**Leading indicators:**
- Number of refined prompts with documented failure modes (shows specification maturity)
- Percentage of documents passing AI validation on first generation (shows prompt quality)
- Team adoption of evaluation frameworks (shows cultural shift)

### Non-Obvious Insights

- **Specification is the new writing skill:** The competitive advantage shifted from "ability to generate prose" to "ability to articulate what prose should accomplish." This is a fundamental skill transition that most organizations haven't recognized yet.

- **Templates hide business logic:** Organizations have been distributing templates for decades without explaining the logical structure underneath. This worked when humans could learn through osmosis but fails catastrophically with AI, which needs the logic explicit.

- **Failure modes are more valuable than success examples:** The presenter emphasizes having "5-7 examples of the kinds of quality problems you have with these kinds of documents." Negative examples teach specification better than positive ones because they reveal hidden assumptions.

- **AI evaluation scales asymmetrically:** Putting AI on the evaluation side (not just generation) creates leverage because one evaluation prompt can assess thousands of documents, while relying on human evaluation creates a bottleneck. "Scaling evaluation means putting AI on the evaluation side, not just the writing side."

- **Ambiguity amplification principle:** Contrary to intuition, AI doesn't reduce ambiguity by adding detail—it makes ambiguous requirements worse by generating plausible-sounding content that appears complete but lacks actual intent. The AI "makes it worse" when adding "helpful" detail to unclear specifications.

- **Information architecture debt comes due:** Organizations have "longstanding information architecture problems in our documents at work that we just paper over." AI writing forces these problems into visibility because AI can't paper over structure problems the way humans could.

- **Voice convergence as signal loss:** "We are converging on voice because of AI and that is leading to informational loss in business systems." The default AI voice is "diplomatically hedged, pseudo comprehensive, stylistically extremely bland" and loses the ability to carry conviction or signal uncertainty appropriately.

- **Evaluation bottleneck is the real constraint:** "We don't have time to evaluate everything that got done, which means that we have to figure out how to scale evaluation." The problem isn't generating more content—it's that evaluation capacity hasn't scaled with generation capacity.

- **Specification forces organizational learning:** By requiring explicit quality standards, AI accidentally forces organizations to develop a competency (articulating intent clearly) that has value far beyond AI writing. This is a strategic capability being built as a byproduct.

- **The bar was always too low:** Organizations tolerated vague documents that didn't serve clear purposes because "you just assumed that people were doing their best thinking." AI makes this tolerance unsustainable, but the underlying problem was always there—humans were just better at compensating for bad specifications.

## 11. Application & Mental Model

### When to Use This Pattern

**Strong signals this approach is needed:**
- Team members complaining about drowning in AI-generated documents
- Decisions getting delayed because documents don't contain the right information
- High variance in document quality across team members
- New employees struggling to understand what "good" looks like
- Meeting notes that don't drive action items to completion
- Multiple rounds of "make it better" iteration with AI without improvement
- Stakeholders asking clarifying questions that should have been answered in the document
- Documents that get written but never referenced for decisions

**Conditions where this approach applies:**
- Organization produces regular business documents (any type: notes, memos, PRDs, press releases, etc.)
- Decisions are made based on documented information (not purely verbal/relationship-based)
- Quality variance is costly (bad specifications lead to bad decisions)
- Scale matters (many documents, many writers, many stakeholders)
- Institutional knowledge is valuable (want standards to outlive individuals)

### When NOT to Use This Pattern

**This approach may backfire when:**
- **Speed matters more than quality:** If the value is in rapid iteration and "good enough" is genuinely sufficient, specification overhead may not pay off
- **Ambiguity is strategically valuable:** Some organizations use vagueness to maintain flexibility or avoid commitment—explicit standards remove this option
- **Creative exploration phase:** When you don't yet know what good looks like, premature specification can constrain valuable experimentation
- **Relationship-driven culture:** If decisions happen through conversation and documents are just ceremonial, investing in document quality is misallocated effort
- **Very small team with tight communication:** If everyone shares context naturally, explicit specification may be bureaucratic overhead
- **Highly individualistic culture:** If the organization values personal expression over consistency, systematic standards create unwanted homogenization

**Warning signs:**
- Team resists specification as "bureaucracy" without understanding the drowning-in-slop alternative
- Leadership wants "AI to just handle it" without investing in requirement clarity
- No willingness to document failure modes (indicates unwillingness to confront quality problems honestly)
- Culture punishes explicit critique of output quality

**General Principles for 1658 Holdings:**

2. **Treat prompts as product requirements:** Use the same discipline for prompt engineering that you'd use for product requirements. Include: purpose, context, required structure, constraints, failure modes, validation criteria. Version control them. Review and refine based on failure cases.

3. **Build evaluation capacity before generation capacity:** Create Claude skills or evaluation prompts for key document types before teaching people to generate with AI. This inverts the typical sequence but prevents the drowning-in-slop problem by establishing quality gates first.

4. **Document failure modes ruthlessly:** When a document fails to serve its purpose, capture it as a failure mode example for the prompt. Make this a team habit: "That's a good failure mode for the PRD prompt." This converts pain into institutional learning.

5. **Measure information-to-decision latency:** For critical document types (board memos, client proposals, strategic reviews), track: how long from document creation to decision, and how many clarifying questions were needed. This reveals where specification investment pays off most.

6. **Use specification to transfer institutional knowledge:** When experienced team members have tacit knowledge about "good" client interactions, itinerary design, partnership negotiations—use the specification process to make that knowledge explicit and transferable. This is succession planning disguised as AI prompt engineering.

7. **Create clear ownership:** Every prompt library, every evaluation framework needs a named owner responsible for refinement based on failure modes. Without ownership, specifications stagnate and the system degrades.

## Strategic Patterns Identified

1. **Specification-Over-Generation Pattern:** When new technology dramatically lowers production costs (AI writing, automation, etc.), the bottleneck shifts from production capacity to requirement clarity. Competitive advantage flows to whoever can articulate specifications most precisely. This applies beyond writing to any domain where AI reduces execution costs to near-zero.

2. **Tacit-to-Explicit Force Function:** AI systems force organizations to make implicit knowledge explicit because machines can't learn through osmosis. This creates painful but valuable organizational learning—converting "I know it when I see it" into documented standards. The pain is the point; it reveals hidden assumptions and quality criteria that were always important but never articulated.

3. **Evaluation-Scaling Asymmetry:** In any system where generation capacity explodes, evaluation capacity becomes the limiting factor. Deploying AI on the evaluation side (not just production) creates leverage because one evaluation framework can assess thousands of outputs. Organizations that master AI-assisted evaluation before AI-assisted generation avoid drowning in low-quality output.
